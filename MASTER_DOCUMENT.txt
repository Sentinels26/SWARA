2. PROBLEM STATEMENT AND THE INVISIBLE GAP
The source project definition identifies the deeper problem as the gap between continuous lived experience and periodic professional assessment.
Wellbeing can change across days and weeks.
A single conversation or one-time assessment can miss longitudinal patterns.
Professionals may not have a structured view of changes between sessions.
Different people have different normal patterns; generic thresholds can create false alarms.
Survivors of atrocities require unusually careful handling of privacy, dignity, consent and safety.
Existing services are valuable, but a monitoring and prioritisation layer can organise changing support needs for human review.
Technology must not turn a survivor into a continuously surveilled data source.
Periodic professional interaction  →  INVISIBLE GAP  →  Consented between-interaction signals  →  Longitudinal view for professional review
NOTE: Positioning SWARA is designed to complement existing services such as Tele-MANAS, DMHP and local professionals rather than replace them.

11. PERSONAL BASELINE ENGINE
Personal baseline is one of SWARA's defining ideas. Instead of assuming one normal range for every survivor, the system learns the individual's typical pattern and looks for persistent deviations.
Historical observations  →  Typical personal pattern  →  Recent observations  →  Baseline comparison  →  Deviation magnitude + persistence  →  Longitudinal change
Example period
Pattern

Stable
Week 2
Stable
Week 3
Slight change
Week 4
Significant / multiple changes
Week 5
Persistent deviation

NOTE: Key mental model The question is not “Is this person outside a generic population threshold?” but “How has this person's state changed compared with their own normal pattern?”
12. LONGITUDINAL CHANGE DETECTION
Look across multiple observations rather than one isolated event.
Compare current patterns with the personal baseline.
Consider persistence and magnitude of deviation.
Use multiple available signals when they exist.
Track missingness/data completeness so absence of data is not silently treated as a negative signal.
Keep explicit immediate safety concerns on a separate pathway.
Baseline  →  Current observations  →  Meaningful/persistent deviation?  →  No → continue monitoring  →  Yes → multimodal fusion → support priority → WHY → professional review

17. SAFETY, SOS, MISSED CHECK-IN AND EMERGENCY OVERRIDE
17.1 Missed check-in
Scheduled check-in missed  →  Predefined protocol  →  Trusted Contact 1 + Trusted Contact 2 receive missed date/time  →  Psychologist receives alert recommending human contact
NOTE: Critical distinction A missed check-in by itself is not proof of an emergency. The workflow must be governed by explicit informed consent and a clearly communicated safety protocol.
17.2 Explicit immediate safety concern
Explicit immediate safety concern  →  Bypass routine scoring  →  Predefined human/emergency pathway  →  Human response
The exact emergency routing must be defined by the real deployment. SWARA should not pretend to be an emergency service.
17.3 Safety controls
Safety pathway must have a humanly accountable owner.
Emergency handling should not depend only on an ML score.
Camera/microphone/wearable permissions must be explicit.
Do not describe every missed check-in as an emergency.
Do not claim certainty about future harm.

25. AI / ML IMPLEMENTATION BLUEPRINT
25.1 Processing pipeline
Raw consented input  →  Validation  →  Normalisation  →  Feature extraction  →  Personal baseline comparison  →  Change detection  →  Fusion  →  Priority  →  WHY
25.2 Text
Extract longitudinal linguistic/contextual features using an appropriate NLP model.
Track changes in communication patterns rather than treating a single word as proof.
Store only the minimum representation required for the defined purpose.
25.3 Voice
Speech-to-text can make voice accessible to the text pipeline.
Optional speech rate, pauses and acoustic characteristics can be considered where technically and ethically appropriate.
Use longitudinal comparison rather than a one-sample conclusion.
25.4 Baseline and change detection
Start with transparent statistical/rule-based methods for the prototype.
Represent baseline, recent window, deviation and persistence.
Keep thresholds configurable and clearly labelled as prototype thresholds until validated.
Do not invent clinical accuracy numbers.
25.5 Fusion
Combine available evidence across consented modalities.
Account for missing data.
Do not treat a missing wearable metric as a negative health signal.
Surface evidence strength and uncertainty to the professional.
25.6 Validation
Professional co-design
Appropriately governed and sourced data
Held-out evaluation
Subgroup/fairness analysis
Calibration
Prospective testing before real clinical use

33. JUDGE Q&A — PREPARED ANSWERS
Q: Isn't this just another mental-health chatbot?
A: No. A chatbot is primarily conversation-based. SWARA is longitudinal: it establishes a personal baseline, monitors meaningful changes, provides explainable support-priority information to a psychologist, recommends personalised wellbeing actions and manages a support lifecycle from referral to professional-authorised closure.
Q: Are you diagnosing mental illness?
A: No. SWARA does not diagnose. It identifies meaningful changes from an individual's baseline and converts them into a support-priority level for human review.
Q: Are you predicting suicide?
A: We deliberately avoid presenting SWARA as a suicide-prediction system. If a survivor explicitly indicates an immediate safety concern, SWARA uses an emergency override and predefined human/emergency pathway rather than waiting for an AI prediction.
Q: Why do you need AI?
A: Information can be distributed across days and multiple signals. AI helps organise longitudinal information, identify meaningful patterns and surface them for professional review.
Q: Why personal baseline?
A: People have different normal patterns. A change that is unusual for one person may be normal for another. Comparing a survivor with their own history makes monitoring more personalised.
Q: What if the AI is wrong?
A: The AI does not independently make a clinical decision. It provides an explainable recommendation to the psychologist, who can accept, reject or reinterpret it.
Q: Why the WHY panel?
A: A professional should not receive a black-box label. The WHY panel shows which signals changed and how they relate to the person's baseline.
Q: Why meditation?
A: Meditation is an optional wellbeing module within the journey, not the clinical decision engine.
Q: Why 50 days?
A: Fifty days is a structured engagement design choice, not a claim that recovery takes exactly 50 days.
Q: Why camera during meditation?
A: The camera is optional and consent-based. Its purpose is basic posture guidance, not diagnosis.
Q: What if the survivor refuses camera access?
A: The survivor can continue using meditation and other SWARA functions without camera access.
Q: Why alert trusted contacts for a missed check-in?
A: A missed scheduled interaction can be a support signal. Under the predefined consent and safety protocol, trusted contacts and the psychologist are informed so a human can decide what to do. A missed check-in alone is not treated as proof of an emergency.
Q: Why two SOS contacts?
A: It provides redundancy if one trusted contact is unavailable.
Q: Can SWARA close a case automatically?
A: No. Closure is human-controlled. The psychologist performs the final assessment and authorises closure.
Q: What is the final report?
A: A Survivor Journey / Wellbeing Completion Report showing progress, milestones, support journey and completion. It is not a medical certificate declaring the person cured.
Q: What if the survivor has a bad day?
A: A single bad day should not automatically produce a high-priority result. The longitudinal system looks for meaningful and persistent changes, while explicit immediate safety concerns use a separate pathway.
Q: How is this different from Tele-MANAS?
A: Tele-MANAS provides an important mental-health support and access pathway. SWARA does not replace it. SWARA adds longitudinal monitoring, personal-baseline change detection, explainability and professional workflow, and can connect survivors to existing services.
Q: What if the survivor stops using SWARA?
A: The predefined missed-check-in workflow can alert the configured trusted contacts and psychologist according to consent and safety settings. The psychologist decides the appropriate follow-up.
Q: What if wearable data is unavailable?
A: Wearables are supplementary. SWARA's core workflow can operate with self-report, text, voice and engagement information.
Q: How do you protect privacy?
A: Through informed consent, data minimisation, controlled access, encryption, secure storage, audit logs and explicit permissions for sensitive sensors.
Q: Can AI replace the psychologist?
A: No. SWARA is explicitly human-in-the-loop. AI identifies patterns and recommends attention; the psychologist interprets, intervenes and closes the case.
Q: Why Python?
A: Python has a strong ecosystem for AI, NLP, speech and data processing. FastAPI gives us an efficient API layer. Keeping the AI and backend in Python reduces integration complexity.
Q: Why Flutter?
A: Flutter gives one mobile codebase for Android and iOS while supporting camera, microphone, notifications and health-data integrations.
Q: How will you validate the AI?
A: With professional co-design, carefully sourced and appropriately governed data, held-out evaluation, subgroup/fairness analysis, calibration and prospective testing before real clinical use.
Q: What is the innovation?
A: The integrated survivor journey: personal baseline, longitudinal multimodal change detection, explainable support priority, personalised wellbeing recommendations, proactive escalation and professional-controlled case management.
Q: How will you scale?
A: The system is modular and containerisable. AI services, APIs, notifications and databases can scale independently. Local-language support and institution-specific referral pathways can be added without rebuilding the core.
Q: Who pays for it?
A: The ethical model is primarily institutional: government programmes, hospitals and NGOs can license the platform. Vulnerable survivors should not be monetised through sale of sensitive data.
Q: What is the biggest limitation?
A: Clinical validity and real-world safety validation. The prototype demonstrates workflow and technical feasibility; real deployment would require professional validation, governance, security assessment, appropriate regulatory review and evidence generation.
34. 60-SECOND PITCH
SWARA stands for Survivor Wellness, Assessment & Rehabilitation from Atrocities. The problem we are addressing is the gap between a survivor's day-to-day wellbeing and periodic professional interactions. SWARA begins with authorised referral and informed consent, gradually understands the survivor's context and establishes their personal wellbeing baseline. Through short daily check-ins, self-reports, optional voice interaction, guided wellbeing activities and compatible health signals, SWARA builds a longitudinal picture of change. Our AI does not diagnose or claim to predict someone's future. Instead, it identifies meaningful deviations from the survivor's personal baseline, explains why they matter and generates a support-priority level for psychologist review. SWARA can recommend personalised, low-risk wellbeing activities and, under a predefined safety protocol, alert trusted contacts and the psychologist when a scheduled check-in is missed. The psychologist remains in control of intervention and case closure. Once sustained improvement is professionally assessed and the case is closed, SWARA generates a Survivor Journey Report documenting progress and milestones. In short, SWARA does not replace the psychologist; it helps the psychologist notice what may otherwise be missed between two human interactions.
